Exam Craft

Four habits for Paper 1A. None of them is about knowing more physics; they are about not losing marks you already have.

1Know your pace, then stop watching the clock

About 90 seconds a question.

HL Paper 1A · 40 questions in about 60 min
SL Paper 1A · 30 questions, a similar pace

Ninety seconds is an average, not a budget for each question. The useful habit is to bank time early: the first easy questions should take you forty seconds, not ninety, and those saved seconds are what let you sit and think on the three or four that deserve it. Running out of time on question 38 is almost always caused by overspending in the twenties, not by that one hard question.

Practise the feel of it rather than the arithmetic. The Pace Trainer hides the clock and just asks you to tap as you finish each question, then shows you afterwards where your time actually went. Set it to 40 questions in 60 minutes for HL, or 30 in 45 for SL.

2Never letter-hunt

“That’s too many Cs in a row” is not information.

Answer keys are deliberately balanced across A, B, C and D. Across the five HL papers of 2025 the letter counts were flat to within a question or two, every time. There is no pattern to exploit and no pattern to fear, so a run of the same letter tells you nothing at all about whether you are right. If your answer is C for the fourth time, and your working says C, write C.

3No theme is safe to skip

All five themes turn up in force, on every paper.

A tempting revision strategy is to gamble: pick the themes that came up last time and skimp on the rest. It does not work here. Every paper draws substantially on all five themes, and topics that vanish from one session reappear in the next. A topic being quiet recently is a reason to expect it, not a reason to drop it.

4Draw it, and commit before you check

The diagram you draw beats the diagram you are given.

When a question describes a situation, sketch it: the forces, the rays, the field, the circuit. Most of the worksheets deliberately leave the diagram to you, because deciding what to draw is most of the physics. A question that feels impossible in prose is often obvious once it is on paper.

The worksheets come with worked answers attached, and that only helps if you write your own answer down first. Many of the questions are built around one specific mistake, so reading the solution before committing gives you the feeling of understanding without the substance of it.

Written for Diocesan IB Physics from an analysis of the 2025 Paper 1A papers.
